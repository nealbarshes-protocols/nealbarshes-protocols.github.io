## Morning report: format and objectives 

We will start at exactly 07:45AM CST. 

The main goal is communicating key updates and other information that will lead to unity of intent and unity of effort among our team members and key partners. 

These updates should include: 
- what _has_ happened since last meeting (not simply the past 24 hours). 
- what _will/should_ happen between now and next meeting, possibly beyond

Communication at this meeting should be current, correct, concrete, clear, concise, and collegial. 

Here is what should be covered:

<hr>

## 1. Inpatient updates _since last morning report_

Objective = Shared awareness of active clinical problems and discharge planning.

### Priority-based ordering of patient presentations: 
1. Any patients with **new complications** & or other **unexpected clinical events** ("critical" or "watchers", in the terminology of [I-PASS](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/ipass.html).
2. ICU patients
3. Inpatients on our vascular teams.
4. Consults that are admitted to other inpatient services.

### The formula for presenting  
ICU patients should be presented in organ-system format by the senior resident responsible for the patient.  

All remaining inpatients, presented by each service's intern or medical student, using this formula:

#### 1. One-sentence summary

One sentence to concisely summarize who the patient is: age/gender, the primary reason for hospitalization, and where in the hospital course he/she is.

_In general, summative like an Axios news headline, not narrative like a Tolstoy novels_. 

Example of a good, concise, plain-language summative first sentence that clearly states basic need-to-know information: "Mr. Smith is a 78 year-old man who had an elective EVAR for a 5.8cm infrarenal aneurysm two days ago."

Example of a cluttered, abbreviation-heavy narrative-type first sentence that has wholly-unnecessary details that obscures the primary reason for hospitalization: "Mr. Smith is a 78 year-old man with hypertension, hyperlipedemia, uncontrolled diabetes, A1c of 7.8, CHF, COPD, GERD, OSA, appendicitis s/p appendectomy, former smoker, an Aquarius, fourth among five children, who presented on 7/11 for EVAR."  


#### 2. Numbered problem list
 
A numbered list of ACTIVE problems should follow this. Clearly state:
1. The **problem**, stated clearly.
2. The **cause**, or at least what is suspected to be the cause, for problems. If unclear, list differential diagnosis and data (ex. labs, imaging findings) that supports the lead cause in the differential.  Cite data as support of statements, not as pointless recitation.  
3. The **plan** for managing the problem

"Active" means new or requiring more than expected management and impacting a patient's recovery or hospitalization in some way. Example: new and severe post-operative nausea may be an active problem if preventing a patient from tolerating an oral diet. Not "headline news" if it is mild. Most chronic medical problems are NOT "active" unless they have some newer or additional problem: ex. acute heart failure exacerbation in someone with chronic systolic heart failure and reduced ejection fraction. 

For patients who have had an operation by our team during this hospitalization, "post-op recovery after ___(operation) for ____(problem)" (NOT " ___ (problem) s/p (operation)" -- there is a difference). 




#### 3. Hospitalization endpoint: goals + discharge planning
Discuss:
1. What needs to happen between now and discharge.
2. How will this happen.
3. Anticipated date of discharge, anticipated discharge destination, and simple/complex categorization.

#### Handling discussion
Answers to yes/no questions should start with either "Yes" or "No".
Answer all other questions directly; don't obfuscate.
Be comfortable saying "I don't know, I'll find out".

Planned admissions between today and next morning report, presented by Nurse Practitioner working on ORC / pre-op planning.
Clinic patient number and coverage for the day.
